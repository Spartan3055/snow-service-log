How to price residential snow removal

Start from what a visit costs you, then decide whether the customer or you will carry the risk of a heavy winter. Published “average” prices are a wide range, not a rate card for your street.

Build a per-push price from your costs

A “push” is one visit to clear the areas named in the agreement. One storm can take more than one push. A straightforward way to price it:

  1. Estimate travel time and on-site time for that property, in hours.
  2. Add labor (your time and any helper), fuel and truck time, a slice of insurance and other overhead, and equipment wear.
  3. If salt or ice melt is included, add the material cost for a typical application.
  4. Divide that visit cost by one minus your target margin. If the visit costs $42 and you want a 30% margin, $42 ÷ 0.70 is $60.
  5. Set a minimum charge so a short driveway next door still covers the roll-out.

Write down the trigger depth, the surfaces, and whether ice control is included or extra. A cheap plow-only price and a “full clear plus salt” price are not the same product.

Seasonal prices need a cap or a buffer

A seasonal price is one amount for the winter, often paid up front or in installments. You are selling the customer a budgeted number, and you are taking the weather risk. If you simply multiply expected visits by your per-push price, a heavier winter can erase the margin.

Common ways to limit that, which you would still put in a written agreement a lawyer has reviewed:

  • A push cap — a set number of visits in the seasonal price, then a per-push overage.
  • A deep-snow surcharge above a stated depth, because one long storm can take several visits.
  • Salt and ice melt billed separately if material costs swing.

Stress-test the number. If your seasonal price assumed 12 visits and the winter brings 20, what is left after costs? The kit’s price-sheet calculator runs a light, average, and heavy case from figures you type in.

Published consumer ranges, for context only

HomeGuide’s page “2026 Snow Removal Prices” (by Tom Grupa, dated December 17, 2024 on the page, retrieved November 19, 2026) publishes these national consumer ranges, among others:

  • Per visit: $30–$70 for a clearance with no additional services
  • Plowing: $30–$100 per visit; an average driveway under 70 feet: $30–$50
  • Hourly (often shoveling or blowing): $25–$75 per hour per worker
  • Salt spreading: $20–$65 per application for 1,000 square feet, with some companies charging $5–$15 per bag
  • Seasonal contracts: $200–$600, described as coverage with an upward limit on visits

The same page also lists a wider “average range” of $44 to $128 and a national average of $81, and it notes that job size, season length, expected snowfall, depth, and time on site all change the number. Treat every figure as a published range, not a quote for your route.

Put the number next to a written scope

A price without a scope is an argument waiting for the first 10-inch storm. Name the surfaces, the trigger, the service window, what happens if cars are in the drive, whether a second pass is included, and how ice is handled.
