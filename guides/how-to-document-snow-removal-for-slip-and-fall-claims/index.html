How to document snow removal for slip-and-fall claims

A visit log will not keep someone from falling, and it will not win a case by itself. It can show, in your own contemporaneous words, when you arrived, what you did, what you used, and what you could not do.

Why the record has to be made at the time

SIMA’s article on effective in-season documentation (retrieved November 19, 2026) puts it plainly: in the eyes of a court or an insurance adjuster, “what is not documented is open for debate.” The same piece says you cannot rely on memory or verbal reports alone, and that “if a service is not recorded, it is as if it never happened.”

A later SIMA piece on slip-and-fall pitfalls makes the same point for mid-season work: unclear or missing documentation “will almost certainly cost you if a lawsuit is filed.” Courts and carriers, SIMA says in its winter contract audits note, place weight on records created at the time of the work, not reconstructed later.

What belongs in a visit log

SIMA’s field-crew guidance in the in-season documentation article says a strong service log is concise and specific, time-stamped on arrival and departure to the minute, and consistent from visit to visit. At a minimum it should include:

  • Service type — for example plowing, shoveling, deicing, or inspection
  • Location or locations serviced
  • Type and amount of materials used (salt, sand, brine, or other products)
  • Arrival and departure times to the minute

A SIMA start-up brief, “How Risky Is Your Business?” (PDF), lists a similar service-report checklist: site name and location; who was on site; date; start and end times; site conditions at start and finish; weather from a verified source; services rendered; materials and amounts; notes on special circumstances; and time-stamped photos.

Document the gaps too. The in-season article tells operations managers that if a property cannot be serviced — a locked gate, unsafe conditions, or another obstacle — you should record the reason and any follow-up. The slip-and-fall pitfalls article adds a practical example: if cars are parked in a lot you were hired to clear, document and photograph that.

What does not belong

The same SIMA documentation article has a “what not to record” list. Do not put subjective impressions in the log (“this looks unsafe,” “the customer is difficult”). Do not record temperature, precipitation, or ice conditions unless you have a reliable source or a measurement. Do not use the service log for complaints about crew members. Do not speculate about fault, negligence, or liability. Stick to actions taken and facts observed.

Weather and photos need a source

SIMA’s “Protect others, protect yourself” list of claims documents includes digital time sheets with before-and-after photos if possible, accurate amounts of salt and other materials, and screenshots or printouts of the forecasts you used to go out. That forecast copy will not be available later in the same form, the article notes; you can look up what the weather did, but not always what you were looking at when you decided to roll.

A practical habit: when you write a weather note in the Storm File log, fill in the weather-source field. The National Weather Service is one public source. An on-site thermometer reading is another. If you did not measure or look anything up, leave the weather fields blank rather than guessing.

A visit log is only part of a claims file

The “protect yourself” list is longer than a phone log: equipment maintenance records, written customer instructions (especially “don’t service that area”), pre-storm messages, site maps, training records, and preseason inspections. Storm File covers the visit itself — times, services, materials, photos, and a reason if you could not finish. It does not replace those other records, and it does not store data off the device. Export a season report or a backup if you need a copy you can file.

SIMA also mentions a retention range of at least three to eight years, depending on local law and insurance requirements. Ask your carrier or a local attorney what applies to you.

How to use the free log for this

  1. Add the property, including surfaces and the trigger depth, before the storm.
  2. Tap Arrive when you pull in. That writes the time.
  3. Record depth if you measured it, the work you did, and material type and amount.
  4. Note areas you did not service and why, plus any follow-up.
  5. Add a couple of photos. They stay on the phone, downscaled.
  6. Tap Depart. At the end of the season, print the property report or download a CSV.
